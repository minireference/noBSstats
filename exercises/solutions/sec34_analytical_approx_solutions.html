E3.38#

Explain in your own words what is wrong with the following interpretations of \(p\)-values:

a) A small \(p\)-value like \(p=0.02\) proves that \(H_A\) is true.

b) The \(p\)-value = \(0.02\) obtained in a clinical trial of a new drug (compared to no treatment) proves that the new drug works.

c) A large \(p\)-value like \(p=0.4\) proves that \(H_0\) is true.

d) The \(p\)-value \(0.02\) tells us the probability that \(H_0\) is true is 2%.

e) The \(p\)-value \(0.02\) tells us the probability that \(H_A\) is true is 98%.

f) When the \(p\)-value is \(0.02\) and we decide to reject \(H_0\), there is only a 2% probability that we made a wrong decision.

g) The \(p\)-value of \(0.02\) tells us that there is a 98% probability that a replication of the experiment will also reject \(H_0\).

h) The tiny \(p\)-value \(0.002\) obtained in a clinical test of a new medication means the medication is very effective.

Hint: Recall the probability calculations used to compute \(p\)-values.

A small \(p\)-value counts as evidence against \(H_0\), but it is wrong to claim that we have proved or definitely shown that the null hypothesis is false. Conversely, a large \(p\)-value means the observed data are not particularly surprising under \(H_0\), but it does not prove that \(H_0\) is true. The logic of hypothesis testing is probabilistic and not absolute: this is why the strong statements in a), b), and c) are all wrong.

The \(p\)-value we compute as part of a hypothesis test is the probability of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the one obtained from the observed data, assuming that the null hypothesis is true. Informally, we can write this as \(p = \textrm{Pr}(\textbf{data}|H_0)\). The \(p\)-value calculation doesn’t calculate the probability of the hypotheses given the data \(\textrm{Pr}(H_0|\textbf{data})\), so the statement d) is incorrect. Similarly, knowing the \(p\)-value doesn’t give us \(\textrm{Pr}(H_A|\textbf{data})\), so e) is also a wrong interpretation. In fact, the data model under \(H_A\) did not enter at all into the \(p\)-value calculation! In general, any statement about the “probability of” \(H_0\) or \(H_A\) is not going to be correct.

The \(p\)-value is not the probability that we made a mistake, so f) is false. In a particular hypothesis test where we reject \(H_0\), we either made a mistake or not, similar to how a particular confidence interval either correctly captures the unknown parameter or doesn’t. The cutoff value \(\alpha\) we use to make our decision is related to the probability of falsely rejecting \(H_0\) across repeated samples, but that doesn’t tell us anything about any particular analysis.

Interpretation g) seems to confuse \((1-p)\) with a probability of replication. The \(p\)-value is not an indication of the magnitude of the effect, so h) is false. In a large sample, it is possible to obtain a tiny \(p\)-value (highly significant result) when testing a drug that has a negligible effect on health.
